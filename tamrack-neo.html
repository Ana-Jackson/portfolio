Program Evaluation

The onboarding had never been measured against its goals. This showed it was working, and surfaced a gap their survey would never have caught.

Background

Tamrack, Inc. is a cleaning supply company with more than 200 employees in five states. Its customers are hospitals and nursing homes. In 2020, the company's Learning and Performance Solutions (LPS) department revised its New Employee Onboarding (NEO) program for virtual delivery. Since then, the program has undergone several revisions, but it had never been formally evaluated. The only feedback the program collected was a single survey at the end of each new hire's first month.

Tamrack's Director of Learning and Performance Solutions asked our team to conduct a formative, goal-based evaluation of the program, which we used Chyung's 10-step evaluation procedure (2019) to complete. We also limited our scope to the structured first-week onboarding experience and set aside pre-boarding activities and the informal one-month integration period that followed.

The NEO program lacked stated performance objectives or named outcomes. Before we could evaluate the program against its goals, we had to establish what those goals were. Working with the client, we used Kellogg's program logic model (2019) to identify the program's resources, activities, outputs, outcomes, and intended impact, then used the model to guide our data collection plan and instruments.

We assessed two dimensions, identified with the client and aligned to the logic model.

Feeling Welcomed

Whether new hires feel welcomed and integrated into Tamrack's culture and values.

Role Readiness

Whether new hires are prepared to begin learning their roles with their managers and teams.

The client confirmed both dimensions as equally important to the organization, so we weighted them equally.

Methods

The program's only existing feedback came from a single survey administered after onboarding ended. A single self-report survey gives a narrow view of how a program is performing. Surveys are also prone to the ceiling effect, where most respondents cluster at the top of a scale and weaken any absolute judgment of quality (Chyung, 2019). So we designed an evaluation that drew on more than one source of data, each chosen to compensate for the others' weaknesses.

This approach is called critical multiplism. We collected data from two stakeholder groups using four instruments. We administered two anonymous, web-based surveys: one to all NEO participants who completed the program in 2025 (27 responses) and one to their managers (14 responses). Surveys let us collect both quantitative and qualitative self-reported data from the full population of both groups, but they can leave out important details, are vulnerable to low response rates, and rely on self-assessment. To offset these limitations, we conducted semi-structured, one-on-one video interviews with three managers and four recent NEO participants, selected through purposive sampling.

We rated quantitative survey items on a 5-point Likert scale: (1) Strongly Disagree, (2) Somewhat Disagree, (3) Neither Agree nor Disagree, (4) Somewhat Agree, (5) Strongly Agree. We coded open-ended survey responses and interview transcripts by theme and sentiment, using a codebook we developed.

Originally, we intended to code open-ended responses by sentiment alone. But during analysis, we recognized that the open-ended survey item ("What is one change you would make to the program?") was framed in a way that invited criticism. This was skewing responses toward negative sentiment. As a solution, we decided to code by both theme and sentiment instead. This gave us a clearer view of what participants were reacting to, not just whether the reaction was positive or negative, and it made our recommendations more actionable.

For each data source, we developed and applied a rubric to assign one of four ratings: Exceeds Expectations, Met Expectations, Improvement Needed, or Serious Problems. We then triangulated the source-level ratings at the dimensional level. We deliberately set a high bar for a dimension to be rated Exceeds Expectations. For a data source to rate Exceeds Expectations, its average had to fall between 4.0 and 5.0 with no respondent selecting either disagree option. And for a dimension to rate Exceeds, at least three of its four sources had to earn that rating independently (or both, where a dimension has two), so a single strong source could not raise the dimensional rating on its own.

"Role Readiness" Evaluation Results

The "Role Readiness" dimension showed strong quantitative performance, with manager survey ratings at 4.68 out of 5, and no Strongly Disagree or Somewhat Disagree options selected on any items. NEO participants rated their own role readiness slightly lower than managers, with a composite participant survey mean of 4.55 out of 5. Self-paced learning was the weakest item (mean 3.63) and the only item in either dimension where respondents selected Strongly Disagree or Somewhat Disagree. Both manager and participant interviews rated this dimension Met Expectations, citing gaps in department-specific systems, benefits knowledge, on-the-job training, content relevance, and post-orientation structure.

"Feeling Welcomed" Evaluation Results

The "Feeling Welcomed" dimension showed strong quantitative results in all data sources. Participant survey responses were especially high, with a composite mean of 4.81 out of 5 and no respondent selecting Strongly or Somewhat Disagree on any welcome-related items. First-week engagement was high as well: 100% of new hires reported meeting with their manager, 92.6% met with their team, and 77.8% met with an assigned mentor. The participant survey rated Exceeds Expectations.

Interviews and open-ended survey responses told a different story. Participants described the orientation as impersonal and reported limited connection outside their immediate team. The survey scores were high, but the welcome experience was uneven. The interviews both rated Met Expectations. Because our triangulation rubric required both sources to rate Exceeds Expectations, we rated the dimension Met Expectations overall. A survey-only evaluation would have recorded this dimension as a top result and missed the gap the interviews revealed.

Conclusions

The NEO program is performing well against its two primary outcomes. New hires report feeling welcome and connected to Tamrack's culture, and managers confirm that new hires demonstrate baseline readiness to begin learning their roles. The program's clearest strength is cultural and relational onboarding. Manager engagement in the first week is universal, team engagement is near universal, and the Day 1 synchronous orientation, values messaging, and team-level introductions are consistently cited as effective.

The clearest area for improvement is the self-paced learning experience. It came up more than anything else in interviews with both participants and managers. Participants described content as too dense for the first week and insufficiently connected to their actual job tasks.

Recommendations in priority order

1

Restructure the self-paced learning experience

Spread content over two to four weeks instead of compressing it into week one. Tailor modules to different roles, and replace some of the passive reading with short exercises based on realistic job tasks.

2

Make mentor meetings more consistent

Not every new hire is getting the same experience, so set a few required check-ins during the first two weeks with simple guidance on what to cover.

3

Help new hires connect outside their own team

The team-level welcome is strong, but new hires described limited connection to the rest of the organization. Adding cross-team introductions or a peer cohort would close that gap.

The evaluation scope was limited to the structured first-week experience, but the data pointed to root causes that cannot be fully resolved within the first week alone. These recommendations address problems identified within the evaluated scope but propose changes that extend into the wider onboarding timeline.

Limitations

This evaluation has several limitations worth weighing alongside its findings. The sample is small: 27 participants and 14 managers responded to the surveys, and the interviews drew on four participants and three managers. The evaluation also covers only the 2025 cohort, and because participants completed onboarding at different points in the year, those who finished in January were drawing on much older memories than those who finished in December.

All of the interviews were conducted by the client rather than by our team. Because respondents knew they were speaking with the person who runs the program, they may have softened their feedback. The interview and survey findings were generally consistent with each other, which partially offsets this risk, but both sources may carry some degree of social desirability bias.

The HR Director was unavailable throughout the evaluation period, so the Director of Learning and Performance Solutions was the sole upstream stakeholder for program context and organizational priorities. That should be kept in mind when interpreting findings related to the program's design intent.

We are confident in the overall dimensional findings, but the sample is too small to support reliable comparisons across departments or quarters. Any patterns along those lines may point to areas worth watching, and should not be treated as definitive.
