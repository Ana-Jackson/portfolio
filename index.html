Work examples

Every project below required looking past the obvious before designing a solution. Start with whichever question pulls you in.

Is it a training problem or something else?
Most of LMNOP Aviation's service centers were missing their productivity targets. Fewer than half were invoicing accurately. The assumption was that people needed more training. The diagnosis told a different story: a mix of environmental and skill factors were driving the gaps, and fixing them meant rethinking the system, not retraining the people.
Is our onboarding actually working?
Tamrack had revised its new employee orientation for virtual delivery and updated it several times since. But no one had ever asked whether it was actually working. Our team evaluated two dimensions that mattered most to stakeholders: do new hires feel welcomed, and are they ready to learn their roles? What we found led to recommendations targeting readiness, team connections, and reduced turnover.
What happens when culture works against strategy?
Nimbus Commerce entered its market as a technical leader. Three years later, competitors had passed it. Revenue targets were being missed. The instinct was to push harder on short-term sales. But six weeks of interviews and artifact analysis revealed something deeper: a culture so tilted toward quarterly results that it was draining the innovation and specialist talent the product needed to compete.
What if clients could gather their own data?
Every time Hale Associates started a new engagement, a consultant had to facilitate the data-gathering process personally. It worked, but it was expensive and it didn't scale. We asked: what if we taught clients to do this themselves? The result was an instructional design that transferred a core consulting skill to the client, freeing up capacity for higher-value work.
How do you onboard 180 new hires across six hospitals?
St. Luke's was about to triple its footprint, opening three new hospitals in two years. That meant onboarding physicians, nurses, support staff, and volunteers at a pace the old in-person approach couldn't handle, in a hybrid work environment. We designed a longitudinal program grounded in adult learning principles and built the business case for an executive who had never worked in L&D.
What happens when the owners can't train everyone themselves?
When Herban Dispensary Group was one store, the owners trained every new hire themselves. With three locations on the way, that wasn't going to work anymore. I designed a blended onboarding with self-paced content and in-store performance support aids. This video artifact teaches bud tenders about terpenes so they can make better product recommendations, which directly drives customer satisfaction and retention.

About

My development as a practitioner

After four years at Apple, I knew something was off, but I didn't have the frameworks to name it. Supporting data annotators and QA teams had shown me that organizational friction kills good technical decisions, but I was still defaulting to training as the fix.

The OPWL program changed that. The frameworks I learned gave me a way to see that most performance gaps aren't knowledge problems. Prosci's research showed me that change adoption depends on readiness conditions, not communication volume. Binder's Performance Chain connected individual behavior to organizational results. And Schein's work on culture embedding mechanisms helped me understand why some changes stick and others don't.

The practitioner identity I've built from those frameworks is centered on one idea: people readiness for change. The work I do now, through Ensyte and through this program, starts with diagnosing whether people have what they need to do what's being asked of them. That diagnosis comes before any intervention design, every time.
