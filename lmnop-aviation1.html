Needs Assessment

$28 million in performance gaps. Leadership asked for training. The data said otherwise.

A systematic needs assessment of LMNOP Aviation's domestic service center network revealed that the root causes were environmental, not individual.

Organization
LMNOP Aviation (pseudonym)
Scope
11 service centers, 1,500+ employees
Frameworks
BEM, Rummler-Brache Nine Boxes
Data Collected
8 interviews, 300+ survey responses

The Challenge

LMNOP Aviation (a pseudonym) is a global aircraft maintenance provider. Its domestic service division has more than 1,500 employees and operates specialized maintenance facilities across the United States. Each service center handles scheduled and unscheduled aircraft maintenance events. These locations are also the organization's primary point of contact with its customers. The division's strategic goals are to improve profitability, operational efficiency, customer experience, and capacity.

Leadership asked our team to conduct a needs assessment after identifying performance gaps in two areas: productivity and invoice accuracy. At the time of the assessment, all but one site were missing goals for productivity, invoice accuracy, or both. The organization's target was 90% of sites meeting both goals. The estimated annual impact from productivity overruns was up to $18 million, with invoice-related losses ranging from $1.6 million to over $10 million. Inconsistent turnaround times made capacity planning difficult, and billing errors damaged customers' trust.

The initiative was sponsored by the Talent and Development Manager, with executive sponsorship from the Vice President of the Domestic Service Center Network. The VP believed that better training for Technical Service Managers (TSMs) and Customer Service Managers (CSMs), along with improved project planning, would be enough to close the gaps. This assumption made the needs assessment especially important. Its purpose was to test whether the problem was, in fact, a training problem.

What leadership assumed

The VP believed that better training for Technical Service Managers and Customer Service Managers, along with improved project planning, would be enough to close the gaps.

What the data revealed

These findings clearly showed that the performance problems were more impacted by organization-wide factors than individual ones. This directly contradicted the initial assumption. Training alone was not going to close the gaps.

Methods

We combined two diagnostic frameworks to structure our analysis. Chevalier's Behavior Engineering Model (2003), a variation of Gilbert's original BEM, categorizes performance barriers into environmental supports (information, resources, and incentives) and individual factors (knowledge/skills, capacity, and motives). The model prioritizes looking at environmental factors first because they are usually easier and more cost-effective to address (Rothwell et al., 2018). This model lent itself well to examining whether individual technicians had the resources and skills they needed to perform their job well. However, during initial conversations with our sponsors and early extant data reviews, we found some evidence to suggest that internal processes may also have been a factor in the performance gap. We added Rummler and Brache's Nine-Variable Framework as a secondary framework, which examines performance at three levels (organization, process, and performer) across three dimensions (goals, design, and management) (Stefaniak, 2020).

Using both frameworks helped us identify barriers and more easily categorize where they originated in the organization. For example, a barrier like "inconsistent feedback" looks different depending on whether it stems from an organizational policy gap, a broken process, or a manager's individual practice. Productivity lent itself to BEM coding because those gaps mapped most directly to performer-level barriers: tools, information, and skills. Invoice accuracy was better served by the Nine-Variable Framework because the problems cut across organizational, process, and performer levels in ways that we may have missed by examining BEM's categories alone.

Data Collection and Analysis

To triangulate data, we collected it from three sources: extant data, interviews, and surveys. First, we examined internal Power BI metrics for each location, including productivity metrics, invoice accuracy rates, schedule adherence, customer satisfaction scores, headcount, attrition, and years of experience across all locations. We used those findings to identify two "exemplary" locations meeting goals, and two "non-exemplary" sites falling short. From there, we conducted 8 semi-structured interviews with TSMs and CSMs at these four sites. By contrasting performance factors at high-performing sites against those at low-performing ones, we could isolate what actually differentiated performance, as well as identify systemic issues impacting all sites. Interview questions were structured around the BEM and the Nine-Variable Models.

We also distributed role-specific surveys across the network to GMs, Maintenance Managers, TSMs, CSMs, and technicians, collecting 300 responses.

To analyze the qualitative data from interviews and open-ended survey questions, we developed a codebook based on both frameworks. When an issue fell into more than one category, multiple codes were applied so that we captured systemic patterns rather than treating each finding as isolated.

Causes

For cause analysis, we used a three-step root cause process. We began by comparing those four sites. From there, Five Whys analysis helped us move past surface symptoms to isolate underlying conditions. Related causes were then grouped into themes and mapped against both target outcomes in a cause-problem overlap matrix. That matrix showed us which root causes affected both problems and allowed us to prioritize accordingly.

The assessment had several constraints. All data collection was remote, so we could not directly observe workflows or team coordination. Time limitations also narrowed our scope. We could only interview at a subset of locations, and support roles such as parts department staff, scheduling coordinators, and IT administrators fell outside our reach. Neither competitive benchmarking data nor compensation data was available. Our recommendations therefore relied on reported practices instead of observed practices. They may need adjustment during implementation.

Root causes clustered into seven themes. Five themes affected both productivity and invoice accuracy, and two were specific to one metric. Across both problems, the issues were inconsistent processes and SOPs, fragmented information and communication, clunky digital tools, uneven training and onboarding, and inconsistent accountability. The two remaining themes were metric-specific. Physical environment and equipment constraints affected productivity at certain sites. Role overload affected invoice accuracy: invoicing responsibilities were layered onto already full coordination roles, which left too little time for careful review.

These findings clearly showed that the performance problems were more impacted by organization-wide factors than individual ones. This directly contradicted the initial assumption. Training alone was not going to close the gaps. Survey results reinforced this finding. The three lowest-scoring BEM categories across the network were information, resources, and knowledge. Although knowledge could be addressed by training, it was not an appropriate solution for information and resource issues.

Key Finding

Differences in performance across sites reflected less about individual effort and more about whether sites are consistently enabled with standard work, usable systems, mentoring, and sufficient role capacity by site leadership.

Intervention Selection

Selecting interventions required us to weigh the impact of the intervention against its feasibility and alignment with LMNOP's strategic goals. We started by using SWOT analysis to identify which root causes would have the greatest impact, evaluating each cause theme's strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats. This told us where the organization already had assets to build on and where the biggest risks lay. From there, we used a Multi-Criteria Analysis to help prioritize potential solutions (Watkins et al., 2011). Rather than selecting interventions in isolation, we met with a core group of General Managers to define the feasibility criteria. Their input produced six weighted dimensions: cost, work stoppage, speed of implementation, sustainability, impact on performance metrics, and number of locations affected. Each potential intervention was scored against these criteria. The scores gave us an objective way to compare interventions instead of relying on our best guess. We organized the resulting recommendations into three levels based on systemic impact.

Recommended Solutions
First level: shared root causes across both metrics

Standardize processes

Standardizing processes meant documenting network-wide SOPs for project planning, scheduling, invoice review, quality control, work allocation, and communication, drawing on best practices from exemplary sites.

Improve tools and systems

For tools and systems, the recommendation was to centralize documentation access, define a single system of record for each maintenance event, and put change management practices in place for system updates.

Formalize knowledge transfer

The knowledge transfer recommendation called for structured mentoring, standardized onboarding for technicians and CSMs, and a competency model with certification pathways tied to technician grade levels.

Second level: more complex to implement

Strengthen performance management and enable peer learning

Strengthening performance management through manager training on consistent feedback, developing career pathways, and enabling peer learning through site champions and role shadowing.

Third level: site-specific infrastructure

Address site-specific infrastructure needs

Tool and equipment issues, parts storage, and hangar capacity.

Implementation plan

The implementation plan would take 12 to 18 months to complete. It started with a cross-functional working group in Phase 0, then moved to standardizing SOPs, with "quick wins" such as an invoice quality control checklist. Tools and knowledge transfer would follow, with later phases adding the remaining recommendations and ongoing evaluation through KPIs and quarterly reviews.
